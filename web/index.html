Chapter 1

Once upon a coal mine

For decades, giant diggers pulled lignite out of the Cottbus-Nord mine. On 12 April 2019 the river Spree was let in.

Radar sees calm water as dark. But look closely: even before the first drop, our radar already counts about 4 km² of "water". The empty pit floor is flat, smooth sand, and to radar that looks almost as dark as a lake. Remember this, we'll come back to it.

Chapter 2

The water learns to dance

Every 12 days Sentinel-1 passes over. For four and a half years the lake grows slowly: dry summers paused the flooding again and again, and the water stayed in separate basins.

In September 2023 officials measured 5.9 km². Our radar says 8.4 km². The difference is mostly that dark sand from Chapter 1.

Chapter 3

One winter, one lake

Then the rain came. Drag the star: between December 2023 and January 2024 our measured lake doubles, from 7.4 to 15.4 km², as the basins merge into one big sheet of water. Officials measured 17.3 km² in February 2024.

On 23 December 2024 the lake reached its target level of 62.5 m above sea level. LEAG then measured 18.5 km², about 2,600 football pitches of brand-new lake. Our radar measures 16.9 km², about 9% low.

Chapter 4

Six years in one picture

Every coloured pixel is lake. Its colour tells you the year it first went under water: violet for the first years, pink and gold for the middle years, aqua for the very last shores.

We only count a pixel once it stays wet on three passes in a row, so a gust of wind or a radar speckle can't fool us. Dark sand can, though: many of the violet 2019 pixels are the pit floor, not real puddles.

Chapter 5

NISAR keeps watch

The lake is full, so NASA and ISRO's new NISAR satellite takes the night shift. Across 17 passes from October 2025 to October 2026 it measures a median of 18.6 km², close to LEAG's 18.5. Careful: the 8 winter passes are beta data, taken before calibration; the 9 calibrated passes alone say 17.6 km².

This is plant view: leaves and branches bounce the radar wave around and turn part of it "sideways". We compare the two radar channels and paint that sideways share green, so green means plants. Drag the star: the bare pit in 2019 on the left, today on the right, with green life creeping back around the shore.

Chapter 6

Why it matters

💧 Water is running out. About half of the Spree's water near Cottbus is groundwater pumped out of the coal mines. When the mines close, parts of the river could carry up to 75% less water in dry summers. Mine lakes like this one will store water for the whole region, so it matters how much water each lake really holds.

⚠️ Shores can give way. Mine-lake banks are built from loose fill. In 2009 a shore at Nachterstedt slid into the lake, taking two houses and three lives. Here at the Ostsee, the noise-protection dam slipped in 2022 and again in 2023.

✦ Radar sees every lake every 12 days, day and night, through clouds, for free. That's a real measurement next to the water plans, and a "look here first" map between expensive survey flights.

Sources: German Environment Agency (UBA) 2023 · LEAG reports to the Brandenburg lignite committee · LMBV

Chapter 7

Don't trust us. Check us.

The diamonds on the chart are official measurements by LEAG, the mine operator. Once the lake is full, our dots land within about 10% of them. While it was filling, sand made us read up to 2.5 km² too high. Windy days (rough, bright water) and wet winter shores (dark, "watery" ground) make single dates jump, which is why we never trust one pass alone.

We taught our water detector using the official shoreline: it labels water vs. land correctly for 92–93% of pixels, with both radar views combined. We've faded the radar here so you can compare with a regular photo from space.

One more honest limit: radar shows where a shore has changed. It can't predict a landslide before it happens.
